<Beside />

Besides <Term id="redemption">redemptions</Term>, a <Term id="liquidation">liquidation</Term> is the one other thing that can settle part of your loan without you. A liquidation costs you part of your deposit, so you want to know exactly what can cause one.

<There /> is the practice vault, set back to your loan as you first took it: 10,000 USDC deposited and 5,000 <Term id="alusd">alUSD</Term> owed. Its <Stat name="ltv">LTV</Stat> reads 50%.

A liquidation can happen once your <Term id="ltv">LTV</Term> reaches 95%, the <Stat name="liq">LIQ LTV</Stat> marker on the <Stat name="bar">health bar</Stat>. From that point anyone can repay part of your loan out of your deposit and collect a fee for doing it.

The person who does is called the liquidator. Liquidations keep every loan in the vault backed by a deposit worth more than the debt.

<ReadOn moment="read-liq-ltv" title="When a liquidation can happen" />

A liquidation takes only part of your deposit: enough to bring your LTV back to 90%, plus the liquidator's fee. The rest of your position stays open and keeps earning.

Between <Stat name="max">MAX LTV</Stat> at 90% and <Stat name="liq">LIQ LTV</Stat> at 95%, nothing is liquidated. In that range you cannot borrow more or withdraw, and the loan stays open.

<Beside />

On most lending platforms, a falling price is what liquidates loans. There you borrow one asset against a different one, so when the collateral's price falls, the <Term id="ltv">LTV</Term> rises while the debt stays the same.

Your loan is borrowed against USDC, so a fall in USDC's price is the risk to test here. USDC rarely moves far from a dollar, but say it fell to 0.90.

<WhatIfMoment />

A price drop on its own cannot liquidate you. The <Stat name="price">USDC price</Stat> fell to 0.90, and your <Stat name="ltv" figure>LTV</Stat> stayed at 50%.

The vault counts both your deposit and your debt in USDC, one USDC of debt for each <Term id="alusd">alUSD</Term> you owe. When USDC falls in dollars, both sides fall by the same share, so the ratio between them holds.

At 0.90, the vault shows your 10,000 <Stat name="deposit" figure>Deposit</Stat> as 9,000 dollars and your 5,000 <Stat name="debt" figure>Debt</Stat> as 4,500, both down 10%. An ETH vault works the same way, with alETH borrowed against ETH.

<ReadOn moment="read-ltv-held" title="Why your LTV held" />

What can raise your LTV, besides borrowing more or withdrawing, is a loss inside the vault. For the second what-if, the <Stat name="price">USDC price</Stat> is back at 1.00, and a <Term id="strategy">strategy</Term> the vault deposits into loses funds worth 10% of the vault.

<Await moment="loss" hint="Press Apply a 10% loss" />

A loss inside the vault lowers your deposit and leaves your debt where it is, so your LTV rises. The strategy's loss lowers what each <Term id="myt">MYT</Term> is worth, so every deposit in the vault is worth less.

Your <Stat name="deposit" figure>Deposit</Stat> fell 10%, to 9,000, while your <Stat name="debt" figure>Debt</Stat> stayed at 5,000, so your <Stat name="ltv" figure>LTV</Stat> rose to 56%.

Your loan is still far from a <Term id="liquidation">liquidation</Term>. From a 10,000 deposit, the vault would have to lose more than 47% of its value before you could be liquidated.

<Work title="How the 47% is worked out">

LTV is your Debt divided by your Deposit. With Debt at 5,000, LTV reaches 95% when your Deposit falls to 5,000 divided by 0.95, about 5,263. That is a fall of about 4,737 from 10,000, a little over 47%.

</Work>

<ReadOn moment="read-liq-steps" />

The liquidation took 5,060 of their deposit, but 5,054 of it paid off their own debt. That 5,054 went to the Transmuter to pay Fixed Yield depositors.

If they repaid the whole loan, they would keep their deposit minus their debt. That amount fell from 1,400 to 394.

The vault's loss cost them 1,000, and the liquidation cost them only the 6 fee.

<Work title="How the 6 fee is worked out">

The liquidator's fee is 1.5% of the amount by which the deposit exceeds the debt. After the loss that was 9,000 minus 8,600, which is 400, and 1.5% of 400 is 6.

</Work>

<ReadOn moment="read-liq-cost" title="What the liquidation cost them" />

If your loan has <Term id="earmarked">earmarked</Term> debt when it is liquidated, the liquidation settles that debt first, out of your deposit. That alone can end the liquidation before anything more is taken.

Settling earmarked debt takes the same amount off your deposit and your debt, as a <Term id="redemption">redemption</Term> does. The amount is a larger share of your debt than of your deposit, so your LTV falls.

If your LTV is then under 95%, the liquidation ends there. A position is liquidated in full only in extreme cases, such as a debt that has reached the value of the whole deposit.

<ReadOn moment="read-earmarked" title="Earmarked debt goes first" />

The lower the LTV you borrow at, the larger the loss the vault would have to suffer before your position reached 95%.

If a loss ever pushes your LTV toward 95%, repaying part of your loan brings your LTV back down, since each dollar you repay lowers your debt.

Above 90% LTV, repay with USDC. A USDC repayment of any size goes through, while an alUSD repayment fails unless the amount brings your LTV back to 90% or under in one go.
